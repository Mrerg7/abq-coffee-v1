Ask ten people how they found their regular cafe and most will describe the same path: they searched a city word and a coffee word. "Coffee near me" after they arrive, "ABQ coffee" before they do. The names that win are the ones already written that way.

## The pattern

The most durable local coffee brands follow a simple formula: **place + category**, sometimes with a third word for texture.

- *Stumptown* — a Portland nickname, a place.
- *Nob Hill* coffee concepts — a neighborhood, a place.
- *Albuquerque Coffee Roasters* — place + category, spelled out.

Each version trades length for memorability. The three-letter city code — **ABQ** — sits in the sweet spot: short enough for a cup sleeve, familiar enough that locals type it without thinking.

## Why category-in-the-domain compounds

When the category lives in the hostname rather than the tagline:

1. **Local search matches end to end.** Google Business Profile name + domain + title tag all carry the query words. Consistency is ranking fuel.
2. **Referrals resolve.** Someone hears "check out ABQ dot coffee" and types exactly that. No "dot com" negotiation, no spelling a made-up word.
3. **Advertising copy writes itself.** The domain is the pitch: city, product, done.
4. **It survives expansion.** A roaster that starts with one cart can still operate the name across wholesale, events, and a future cafe without rebranding.

## What to avoid

- **Invented words** — pronounceable but unsearchable until you spend a fortune teaching the market.
- **Overlong strings** — three-word domains die on signage and in voice search.
- **Generic .coms that hide the place** — `elitebeans.com` could be anywhere; nowhere is an advantage only for national players.

## The test

Say the name out loud on a phone call. Can the other person type it without a follow-up question? Does it show the city? Does it show the product? **ABQ.Coffee** passes all three — which is why it is [offered at $100,000 via Escrow.com](/#acquisition).
