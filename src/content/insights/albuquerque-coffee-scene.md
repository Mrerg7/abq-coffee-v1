Albuquerque's coffee map is not one scene — it is several, stitched together by the Tramway, the river, and I-25. A brand that claims the city name has to understand how the map is actually drawn.

## Nob Hill and the University

Route 66 through Nob Hill is the densest stretch: independent espresso bars, patio seating that fills at 8 a.m., and steady foot traffic from UNM. This is where third-wave language and oat-milk defaults live. A short, design-forward name works here because the audience already reads names for a living.

## Downtown and the Sawmill

Newer build-out, office workers by day, event traffic by night. Cafes here lean toward throughput and all-day hours — drip that holds, pastry that survives until noon, a space you can laptop in. The brand on the window should say *city* first; the neighborhood is assumed.

## The Northeast Heights

Suburban drive-thrus and strip-center stalwarts. Loyalty is habitual, orders are repeat, and the name mostly needs to be easy to say through a speaker. Three letters and a dot do that better than a sentence.

## The West Side and Rio Rancho

Fastest-growing rooftops, thinnest independent-cafe density — mostly chains and drive-up windows. This is where a local identity has the most room to arrive as something other than a franchise logo. A city + category name imported here reads as "the Albuquerque coffee company," which is exactly the positioning.

## Roasters selling wholesale

Several ABQ roasters distribute bags to cafes, offices, and gyms across the metro. For them the domain does double duty: consumer discovery on one side, wholesale credibility on the other. City + category on the label means a buyer in Santa Fe or Denver knows the origin story before opening the bag.
