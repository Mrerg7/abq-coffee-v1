Albuquerque sits near 5,300 feet. Coffee recipes written at sea level do not survive the climb unchanged. Here is what actually changes, and how shops above 5,000 feet adjust.

## What the altitude does

**Water boils lower.** At 5,300 feet, water boils around 202°F (94.5°C) instead of 212°F. You cannot pour boiling water at altitude the way a sea-level recipe assumes — the ceiling is lower before you start.

**Boils off gas faster.** Lower air pressure means dissolved CO₂ leaves roasted beans and brewed coffee more quickly. Freshly roasted beans degas faster in the bag; blooming a pour-over behaves differently; crema on espresso collapses sooner.

**Extraction runs cooler and faster.** A pour that would extract fully at sea level can under-extract here: the slurry sits a few degrees cooler, and the grind that produced a balanced cup in Portland may taste sour and thin on Central Avenue.

## How Albuquerque shops adjust

1. **Grind finer.** The single biggest fix. More surface area compensates for the lower slurry temperature.
2. **Push water hotter — to the local ceiling.** Use water just off the local boil rather than the 200°F a low-altitude guide suggests.
3. **Restroast-aware.** Beans reach peak flavor sooner at altitude; tighten the roast-to-brew window and date bags honestly.
4. **Re-dial espresso daily.** Barometric swings with weather systems move shot times more at elevation. A recipe card from last week is a starting point, not gospel.
5. **Taste for sweetness, not time.** Fixed extraction percentages matter less than the cup in front of you when the physics have changed.
